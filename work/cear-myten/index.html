Work / CEAR ·Mission organization

A discipleship app that remembers every person a missionary is walking with

CEAR gave its missionaries in Asia one private place for the people they disciple: stages, prayer, dated notes and progress, on a phone that works without signal.

Client
CEAR
Where
Asia
What we built
MyTen, a mobile app: disciple stages, prayer and study tracking, dated notes, progress, statistics, passcode lock

What they came with

Missionaries do not work with an audience. They work with ten or twelve people by name, over months, and the whole of that relationship normally lives in a notebook, a notes app and a memory of who was asked what. It holds up until the notebook fills, the phone is replaced, or the missionary moves to another field and has to hand twelve people to somebody else.

What was getting in the way

The gap was never motivation. A missionary can tell you exactly where each of their people stands this week. Six months later, nobody else can: which prayer requests were real, who was already studying, who stopped coming after the third visit and why.

The record is also sensitive. A list of names of people studying the Bible is not an ordinary contact list in a country where it can cost those people something, so whatever holds it has to be at least as careful as the person carrying the phone.

And discipleship has a shape. A ministry does not manage leads; it walks people through stages — trust before the encounter, the encounter before study — and a general-purpose contacts app flattens all of that into one list with a checkbox.

What we built

MyTen, a mobile app built around people rather than records. Every disciple has a card: a photograph, how long you have walked together, and which of the ministry’s own stages they are in — winning trust first, then the encounter, then what follows. Moving someone forward is a deliberate act, done by the missionary, not a status that changes itself.

Under each person are the four rhythms the ministry already works in — pray, connect, fellowship, study — each with a target and a count. A missionary can see in one screen which of their twelve people have not been prayed for this week and which have not been visited this month.

Notes are dated and kept as a history rather than one field that gets overwritten, because the worth of a note from March is that it was written in March. Statistics roll the same data up by disciple and by activity, which is what makes a handover possible at all: whoever takes over inherits the story, not a list of names.

The app opens behind a passcode. That was a requirement rather than a feature, and it shaped what the app is allowed to keep and what it deliberately does not. The design had to survive being used standing up, outdoors, in a hurry, by someone who is not thinking about software.

For an organisation that needs the same picture at the level of a field rather than a single missionary, this is the personal-scale version of the member and discipleship CRM work.

What this means for a church like yours

A tool for missionaries succeeds or fails on whether it matches the way the ministry already disciples. If the stages in the app are not your stages, people keep the notebook and fill the app in afterwards, badly, and within two months the data is worth nothing to anyone.

So start from the method rather than the software: write down the stages your people actually walk through, and what a missionary is meant to do at each one. If that is not written down anywhere yet, that is the first thing to fix — with or without an app.

What this drew on

  • Mobile app development
  • Member and discipleship CRM
  • Product design

Questions about this project

Could our mission field use the same app?

The structure transfers; the stages usually do not. Every ministry disciples in its own order, and the first thing we do is map yours instead of imposing CEAR’s. If your method is close, the work is configuration and language. If it is different, the app is the same shape with your stages inside it.

How is the information about people kept private?

The app opens behind a passcode on the device, and we kept the record as small as the ministry can work with: what is not collected cannot leak.

Who maintains it after launch?

The ministry owns the app and the account it ships from. We stay available for the work that genuinely needs us — platform updates, a new stage in the method, a new field — and not for the work that does not.
